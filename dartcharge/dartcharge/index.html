Where it is located

What is Dart Charge?

A free flow toll on the Thames crossing, where nothing on the road stops to tell a driver a charge exists.

Dart Charge is located on the Thames river, on the border of Kent, London and Essex.

The Dart Charge is in place to manage demand rather than to pay for infrastructure. The Crossing was designed to handle 135,000 vehicle crossings a day, but currently it is not uncommon for 160,000 vehicles to make the trip. Research from 2001 indicates that lifting the toll could lead to a 17% rise in traffic.

Free flow gantries replaced the barriers in 2014. Cameras read the plate at speed, so nothing stops to tell the driver a charge exists.

Secondary research

What is happening

What we could learn without users: the product itself, the screens missing from it, the standards it breaks, the competition, and what people say unprompted.

What we set out to prove

Research objectives

This discovery phase was intended to highlight any improvement to be made to the service, and to ultimately improve customer service and satisfaction.

Objective 01Check what works, and what people still need.

Before talking to anyone, we read the whole service

Two weeks with the live site, the guidance, the letters and the call centre notes. It set the questions worth asking people, and turned up a set of screens that never existed.

Summarised below

The remaining six findings, one line each: two more major problems and all four irritants. Two irritant cards carried identical recommendations, so the duplicate is not repeated here.

Major problem H3, H5, H10 The number of steps in payment and account setup is never shown, and a single colour is used for every state, so nothing says where you are. Show system status with colour, and label every step.
Major problem H4, H5 Two names for one task, create an account and set up an account. A yellow highlight used for updates reads as a payment notice, and traffic updates look clickable. One vocabulary across account creation, and colour reserved for meaning.
Irritant H3, H5, H10 On the no charge screen a person has to log in before paying, which removes their control, and account creation offers no way out once started. Let people pay without an account, and give every flow an exit.
Irritant H1, H4, H8, H10 Balance and account number look identical to every other field, a green highlighted field looks like a button, and data is cluttered rather than grouped. Weight what matters. Bold the balance and account number, group the rest.
Irritant H4, H5, H10 Back and next buttons change position and appearance between tasks, and add or remove buttons move, so anyone new to the service struggles. Fix button positions once, and keep next and back side by side throughout.
Irritant H3, H5, H7, H10 The current stage is unreadable except on the account setup page, task mapping is not apparent, and a flat interface adds visual confusion. Make the stage and the task mapping explicit on every screen.

Ten heuristics, three severity bands, nine distinct findings from ten cards. Once a problem had a grade attached, the conversation stopped being about opinion and started being about order.

Method 06 · card sorting and affinity mapping · group task

Six scenarios where the service and the driver disagree

Everything we had heard went onto a shared board as individual notes, then was sorted until clusters held. Six scenarios came out of it, and all six are moments where the system believes one thing and the driver believes another.

Scenario 01Late payment, first contravention, paid within 14 days
Scenario 02Paid late, not a first contravention or more than 14 days late
Scenario 03Paid wrong or incorrect VRM, non account
Scenario 04Negative account balance, first report
Scenario 05Made X number of crossings, but only paid for Y
Scenario 06Vehicle not registered to the account at the time of crossing

Pain points

A user may be paying after the midnight cut off discretionary period and should therefore not proceed with the payment, as it will only contribute towards a future crossing.

No upfront notification that a PCN is being issued for late payment. We understand this can take up to five working days to issue the initial warning letter.

This heightens the risk of surprise and anxiety felt by a user who may be completely unaware that they have paid outside of the discretionary payment period.

Future improvement opportunities

Notify users that they have made a late payment, outlining why it constitutes a late payment, before explaining what happens next.

Say when users can expect to receive the letter, and any markings on the envelope to watch for, so they can act on receipt.

Explain the reasoning behind the five working day window before the letter is issued.

Method 07 · user personas · group task

Eight personas, built on behaviour rather than demographics

The eight personas being made as part of this project are behavioural based. Behavioural based personas focus on a key behaviour of the user as they interact with the overall service. This means that any one user could be true of multiple personas, depending on their context of service use at any time, or the wider work and social context they may find themselves in.

01The anxious challenger 05The careful planner 02The wilful fine payer 06The free time traveller 03The diligent company reconciler 07The leave and let go local resident 04The busy self employed professional 08The family administrator

Each persona carries context and emotion, goals and priorities, emotional and practical needs, the Dart Charge touchpoints they actually meet, and a future opportunity. Two are shown in full below.

What the set had in common

Nobody wanted an account. Across all eight, the account is a means to an end, and in two cases it is the thing they are trying to avoid.

Every practical need is informational. How long the process takes, what the charge is, when it is due, where to go. None of it needs new data.

The touchpoint lists are short. Website, contact centre, PCN letter, email. Three of the four only appear after something has gone wrong.

How they shaped the design

Reminders before the deadline, because both personas shown here ask for them in different words.

The obligation and the exact date on the first screen, since “information up front how long the process is” is a stated need, not an inference.

Paying and challenging offered with equal weight, because a persona whose goal is to not pay still deserves a route.

Method 08 · user journey map · group task

Planning a journey and using the service for the first time

The other task were the user journey maps. There is one map, focusing on the current ‘as is’ user experience and using insights drawn from all of our research. The map outlines a user’s approach to planning a car journey and then using the crossing for the first time.

Where the severity peaks

Might forget to pay for the crossing. The tallest peak on the map, and it sits at the one step where the service has no way to reach the driver at all.

Might navigate to a third party payment site and pay a higher charge for using the crossing.

Might not be expecting to pay any road user charge on this journey. The failure begins two days before the crossing.

What the map made obvious

Every touchpoint before the crossing is Google, a road sign or nothing. The service only appears once the driver already owes money.

Dart Charge takes no action at all for the first four steps, while the user takes seven.

The data captured at the crossing is the registration, make and model, date and time. Enough to tell the driver what they owe, and none of it shown to them.

Method 09 · service map · group task

The end to end service, including everything the driver never sees

This document maps the end to end service for Package A, including user experience and back end processes. This is the to be experience, rather than the one that was in place in 2020. It considers one type of journey, not multiple journeys, and therefore misses those who create an account before crossing.

Day 1CrossingThe user sees road signs detailing the distance to the crossing, charge operating hours and the payment deadline, then takes the crossing. Roadside equipment captures an image of the vehicle with registration, date and time, and the back office looks for an account.
Day 2Account creationThe user navigates to the website, learns what the charge costs, deliberates between a one off payment and an account, then enters personal and vehicle information. Account holders and automated pay as you go users receive an email confirming payment and balance.
Day 2The deadlineThe user fails to pay by midnight after day two. Either no payment or account is found, or an account is found but suspended due to insufficient funds.
Days 3 to 4Account managementAccount holders can top up, at which point unpaid crossings are paid and the suspension is removed. Nothing in the service prompts them to do it.
Day 5EnforcementThe registration is marked as a contravention candidate and forwarded to the image review team. The keeper is identified via DVLA and a penalty charge record is generated, which persists throughout the entire enforcement process. The journey continues within Package B.

What the map exposed

Five days from compliant to enforcement, and the service speaks to the driver on none of them except through a letter that arrives at the end.

The back end knows before the user does. The database scan, the DVLA lookup and the suspension all happen silently while the driver assumes they are covered.

Suspension is recoverable and nobody says so. Topping up clears the unpaid crossings, but the only alert arrives when it is already too late.

What that changed in the design

Everything the redesign does sits inside days one and two, because after that the cost multiplies for both sides.

Account health became a visible state rather than a back end one, so the driver learns what the system already knows.

The alert moved earlier: warned at 30, 7 and 1 days, not once the account is already suspended.

Method 11 · gap analysis, through usability tasks · solo task

Gap analysis

A gap is the distance between what someone came to do and what the service let them do. The only reliable way to measure it is to watch someone try. Three usability tasks produced the candidates, and four other methods decided which of them were real.

How the gaps were found

Three scenarios put participants in front of the live service with real intent, so a gap had to be watched rather than reported. Where someone hesitated, backtracked or gave up became a candidate gap, then was checked against the interviews, the heuristic review, the IA walk and the public complaints.

01Planning a journeyShow how you would usually plan an upcoming car journey. Tolls were never mentioned, to see whether they came up unprompted.
02Paying a crossingExplored non account holders’ awareness of the service, and how they would naturally search for and pay a charge.
03Account managementFocused on how existing account holders access and use their account, and what they believed it could do.

What the analysis settled

Listed together, the seven stopped reading as seven problems and started reading as one. The service already held everything it needed: the crossing, the registration, the date, the balance, the card expiry. What it did not do was say any of it out loud, at a moment when saying it would have changed the outcome.

The brief changedThis is the point the project stopped being a payment redesign and became an obligation redesign. Faster forms would not have moved a single one of these seven.
The order changedFour of the seven sit at or before payment, where the service had least presence and the driver had most to lose. That is why the first screen was redesigned before anything else.
The scope heldNo gap needed data the crossing does not already hold, and none needed a policy change. Every one was answerable in the interface, which is what made the roadmap deliverable.
What stayed outCandidates named by only one method were held back rather than designed for, including several from the usability sessions. A single sighting is a hypothesis, not a gap.

The challenge

A toll with no tollbooth, and a rule nobody is told

Drivers needed to pay a small urgent debt from a phone, and the service asked them to open an account first.

The Dartford Crossing removed its barriers in 2014. Cameras read plates at 50mph and up to 160,000 vehicles pass on a busy day. Nothing on the road stops to say that a charge exists, that it is due by midnight the following day, or where to pay it.

Drivers needed to settle a small, urgent debt from a phone, often within hours. The service instead asked them to choose an account type, register, and understand a deadline expressed as “the day after you cross”.

Most people met the system for the first time through a penalty notice. Fines came from confusion, not from cheating: users did not know a charge existed, when it was due, or where to pay it.

The tension: drivers needed to pay a £2.50 debt in under a day. The service asked them to open an account first.

Step 01You cross at 50mphNo booth, no barrier. Many first time drivers do not know they have to pay at all.
Step 02Pay by midnight tomorrowRoughly 24 to 48 hours. Roadside signs are the only warning most drivers ever see.
Miss it£2.50 becomes £70The penalty is posted to the registered owner. Ignore it and it climbs to £105.

Goals and success

Fewer fines, fewer phone calls

A penalty is a failure for both sides: the driver pays twenty eight times the charge, and the operator processes an appeal, a letter and often a phone call. Success is a driver who pays on time without ever contacting anyone.

Research and discovery

Fines came from confusion, not from cheating

People did not know a charge existed, when it was due, or where to pay it, so the brief moved from payment to obligation.

1

People did not know a charge existed

Contact centre themes were dominated by “I did not know I had to pay” and “when was it due”. For many drivers the penalty notice was the first message the system ever sent them. That reframed the brief from payment UI to obligation communication.

2

Two different jobs shared one journey

“Pay a crossing” and “manage my account” were funnelled through the same steps, and the funnel review showed most users leaving at choose account type, before they could start paying at all. One journey was hurting both audiences.

3

The moment of payment is mobile and stressed

People search “pay dartford crossing” from a lay by or the sofa the same evening, on one bar of signal, sometimes minutes before midnight. Every extra tap in that session carries a £70 risk, so the design target was the worst conditions, not the ideal ones.

Design direction

Four principles shaped every decision

Task before account, deadlines as design elements, no silent states, and self service instead of the phone queue.

Task first, account laterThe urgent job is “I just crossed, let me pay”. Sign up comes after payment, never in front of it.
Deadlines are design elementsThe most consequential fact in the service gets a component, not a line of guidance.
No silent statesNothing about the account may be hidden. Every problem is visible, warned early, fixable in place.
Self service replaces the queueEvery common support question earns a screen that answers it without a phone call.
Ideation and trade offs

What I rejected, and what it cost to reject it

Four options were turned down, each one cheaper to build and worse for the person paying.

Considered · one unified wizardEveryone enters the same flow, splitting into guest or account near the end. Cheapest to build and a single code path.
Chosen · split entryQuick pay is the primary action, accounts are a separate path. It duplicates some components, which a shared library absorbs. The alternative repeated the step where users abandoned.
Considered · a countdown timer“7 hours 42 minutes left” is dramatic and tested well in isolation.
Chosen · an exact date and timeA countdown is unusable to a screen reader and meaningless in a screenshot. “Due by 23:59, Sun 26 Jul” survives being read aloud or seen a day later.
Considered · full account lookupLet anyone enter a plate and see the account, payments and history behind it.
Chosen · crossing status onlyThe check returns whether a crossing is paid, due, or not recorded, and nothing personal. That is what lets it work with no login.
Considered · a separate hire car taskA dedicated route for drivers paying on a vehicle they do not own.
Chosen · remove itIt duplicated the ordinary payment journey, which never asks who owns the vehicle.
The approach

Six decisions, each answering numbered problems

Every decision names the failure points it answers, the constraint that shaped it, and the screen it produced.

01

The first screen states the obligation, not the login

Problem, goal, design
Problem · points 01, 04, 05The most urgent job in the service sat behind account steps. One off drivers had to understand account types before they could pay £2.50, and the old journey ran to six steps with registration first.
Design goalA stranger to the system pays a crossing in under two minutes, with zero prior knowledge.
DesignObligation, then a computed deadline with the penalty named, then one green action. Logging in is a link at the foot of the screen for the minority who have an account.
ConstraintGOV.UK branding is mandatory and I kept the black header and yellow focus states exactly. It also helps with point 05: the official style is hard for fake payment sites to copy.
Before and after
Before · six steps
Landing→ Choose account type→ Register and verify→ Add vehicle→ Payment set up→ Pay
Registration came before payment, and most users quit at the second step, before they reached a payment field.
After · four screens
1 · Vehicle→ 2 · Crossings→ 3 · Pay→ Receipt
Payment comes first. Signing up is offered on the receipt, to the people it actually saves money.
LandingObligation, the dated deadline, one action.
1 · VehicleA white UK front plate field, UK or non UK.
2 · CrossingsWhat the cameras read, each with its own deadline.
ReceiptA reference to keep, then the account offer.
02

The deadline became a component with four states

Problem, goal, design
Problem · points 02, 03The entire penalty system rests on one rule, and it existed only as a sentence. Users could not turn “the day after” into a date, and paying for the wrong day failed silently.
Design goalNo user can move through the service without knowing exactly when payment is due, as a date, not a rule.
DesignOne component, computed from the device clock, repeated in six places: landing, plate step, each crossing card, the payment screen beside the amount, the receipt, and the notice.
The deadline, in four states
Due by 23:59, Sun 26 JulUpcoming, the exact date from the start Due today by 23:59The last safe moment, made loud Past deadline, see what to doNever blames the user, links to help Paid · ref DC 48291The worry ends here, with proof
AccessibilityStatus is never colour alone. Each state carries an icon, a colour and words, so it survives colour blindness, greyscale printing and being read aloud.
Plate stepBoth deadlines shown as exact dates.
Payment stepThe deadline chip sits beside the amount.

The exact date rule. “Due by 23:59, Sun 26 Jul”, never “by tomorrow”. The component does not allow vague wording, and the system does the date arithmetic. Anyone paying in advance can ask for an email reminder before the deadline.

03

“Have I already paid?” answered in thirty seconds, with no account

Problem, goal, design
Problem · point 04It is 11:40pm, the deadline is twenty minutes away, and the driver thinks they paid. The old answers were log in, with no account, or call, with the lines closed.
Design goal Anyone can find out whether a crossing is paid in under a minute, without an account.
DesignBecause the check returns crossing status only and nothing personal, it needs no login at all. Three outcomes, all of them honest: paid, with the reference and date, proof in hand if a notice arrives by mistake; due, with the date and a button into payment with the crossing already selected; not found, honest about camera delay, with both innocent reasons named and a manual route.
PaidReference and date, proof in hand.
DueThe date, and a route straight into payment.
Not foundCamera delay named, with a manual route.
04

A dashboard that cannot stay silent

Problem, goal, design
Problem · points 06, 07, 08, 11Accounts closed quietly, cards expired without warning, and drivers were fined with money sitting in the account, because nothing on the surface said the money was not reaching the crossings.
Design goal No account holder is ever surprised by a charge their balance should have covered.
DesignOne card carries the account’s state in three forms, warned at 30, 7 and 1 days before a card expires.
Account health, in three states
ActiveYou are covered. Crossings are paid automatically. Action neededYour card expires in 12 days, auto top up will stop working. SuspendedYour crossings are not being paid. You could receive penalties.

Auto top up is shown as a state, and off is styled as a warning.

DashboardReady to cross, with auto top up visible.
Balance activityEvery movement, with the running balance.
Behind itA ledger where every top up, charge, refund and failed payment is one row with the balance after it and a retry where it applies, so the number on the card is always explainable.
05

Paying and challenging, offered equally

Problem, goal, design
Problem · points 13, 14, 15Appeals went into a paper form and a phone queue, with no reference and no way to see whether anyone had read them, while the fine grew during the wait. Being heard carried a price.
Design goal Challenging a penalty costs nothing and always returns something to track.
DesignThe notice states the reduced amount, the full amount and the date each applies until, and neither route is buried. A challenge names the accepted grounds, takes evidence, and returns a case reference with a 28 day reply window. The reduced amount is held while the case is open.
The noticeReduced and full amounts, both dated.
Challenge submittedA case reference and a 28 day window.
And point 15Address changes live in the account, so notices follow the owner instead of an address they left years ago.
06

Two dead ends, given a way out

Problem, goal, design
Problem · points 16, 18Cameras can take an hour to record a crossing, so “not found” read as “nothing owed”. A plate the DVLA cannot match ended at a validation error. Foreign drivers know the rules least and are least able to ask.
Design goal No driver reaches a dead end, whatever plate they have or how new the crossing is.
DesignWhere no crossing is recorded yet, the app explains the delay and takes payment in advance, matching it later. Where a plate cannot be matched, a self declaration collects make, model, colour and class, so nobody is stuck at an error. A language switch and a contact screen that leads with webchat and a callback, stating the current phone wait honestly, close the rest.
Pay in advanceWhen the cameras have not caught up yet.
Self declarationFor a plate the DVLA cannot match.
Mobile craft

One hand, one bar of signal, twenty minutes left

The design target was the worst conditions of the session, not the ideal ones.

The deadline never scrolls awayIt travels with the amount and the navigation context, never below the fold.
The plate as a physical objectWhite field, black type, plate letterforms and the right keyboard. People type what they can see on the car.
Thumb zone architectureOne question per screen, the action pinned to the bottom, targets well above the minimum.
Less typing everywherePlate lookup, postcode lookup, card scan, numeric keyboards. Less typing means fewer errors.
Interruption resilienceCalls, notifications, tunnels. Progress is kept and resumable, because “start again” costs £70.
The stressed wrong tapTargets larger than the minimum, and destructive actions kept away from where the thumb rests.

Validation and iteration

Five things review changed

The prototype went through three rounds of critique against the HIG and GOV.UK content standards, plus a coverage audit that traced each of the eighteen problems to a screen.

BeforeA green pill sat behind the selected tab. AfterTint colour and the filled icon variant only. iOS never fills a tab item, and the pill made the bar read as a web nav.
BeforeYellow plate fields, copied from rear number plates. AfterWhite with black type, the UK front plate, which is the one a driver photographs or reads while standing in front of the car.
BeforeA “Who is the account for?” step with personal and business options. AfterDeleted with the business option. A question with one answer is not a question, so verification now leads straight into payment setup.
BeforeA low balance banner that had to be switched on by hand. AfterThe state raises its own banner, with the exact top up amount in the action. A warning that depends on someone remembering to show it is not a warning.
BeforeA separate “Pay for a hire car” task in the menu. AfterRemoved. It led to a journey identical to ordinary payment, so it cost comprehension and bought nothing.

Reflection

What I would do differently

Two lessons, one hard call, and one habit worth keeping.

Do differently

Find the failure before fixing the screen

Two weeks went into the payment form. The real gap was that nobody knew a payment was due.

Do differently

Finish what you cut

Removing the hire car route was right. One line of copy should have replaced it.

Hardest call

Leaving fleets out

Still right. Nobody reconciles forty vehicles on a phone.

Keep
