GOV.UK service · iOS concept · Research with a client team, design solo

Dart Charge, redesigned around the rule nobody is told.

The Dartford Crossing charges every driver £2.50, but nothing on the road says so, or that it is due by midnight the next day. Miss it and it becomes £70. I worked on the research that found why people were fined, then designed an iOS service that tells them in time.

The Dartford Crossing. Barriers came down in 2014, cameras read plates at speed.

Context

A toll with no tollbooth, and a deadline written as a rule.

Cameras read every plate at 50mph. Nothing stops the driver to say a charge exists, when it is due, or where to pay. The rule lives in guidance as “the day after you cross”, and most people meet the service for the first time through a penalty notice.

160,000

Vehicles on a busy day, at a crossing built for 135,000.

£2.50 → £70

The cost of paying one day late. Ignored, it climbs to £105.

277,000

Penalty notices a month at the 2024 peak.

Penalty figures are 2024 public reporting (Fleet News, National Highways). They frame the problem, they are not part of the research.

Research

Fines came from confusion, not from cheating.

Eleven methods over three blocks: auditing the live service and six international comparators, reading what people posted unprompted, more than 50 interviews across eleven user groups, then personas, journey maps and a gap analysis. The full method is in the appendix. Three findings shaped everything after it.

01People did not know a charge existed.

The most common complaint was “where was I supposed to find this out”. For many drivers the penalty notice was the first message the system ever sent them.

02Two different jobs shared one journey.

“Pay a crossing” and “manage my account” ran through the same steps, and most people left at “choose account type”, before they reached a payment field.

03The moment of payment is mobile and stressed.

People search “pay dartford crossing” from a lay by or the sofa, on one bar of signal, sometimes minutes before midnight. Every extra tap carries a £70 risk.

7

Gaps confirmed by two or more methods. Single sightings were held back.

4

Of them sat at or before payment, where the service was least present.

0

Needed data the crossing does not already hold. Every gap was communication.

Reframe

The brief said payment. The real job was the obligation.

The service already held the crossing, the plate, the date, the balance and the card expiry. It just never said any of it out loud at a moment when saying it would change the outcome. Faster forms would not have moved a single gap.

Tell people what they owe and by when, before it becomes a fine.

Task first, account later.

The urgent job is “I just crossed, let me pay”. Signing up comes after payment, never in front of it.

Deadlines are design elements.

The most consequential fact in the service gets a component, not a line of guidance.

No silent states.

Nothing about an account may be hidden. Every problem is visible, warned early, and fixable in place.

Self service replaces the queue.

Every common support question earns a screen that answers it without a phone call.

Decisions

Six decisions, each answering a numbered problem.

The research became eighteen numbered failure points. Every screen had to name the points it answered. The phones below are the live prototype, not images.

01 · Obligation before login

The first screen states what you owe, not where to log in.

Problem

One off drivers had to understand account types before paying £2.50, and registration came first.

Design

The obligation, a computed deadline with the penalty named, then one green action. Logging in is a quiet link at the foot.

02 · The deadline component

The deadline became a component, with four states.

Problem

The whole penalty system rested on one rule, written as a sentence. People could not turn “the day after” into a date.

Design

One component, computed by the system, repeated in six places, from landing to receipt. Never “by tomorrow”, always a date.

03 · Have I already paid?

“Have I paid?” answered in thirty seconds, with no account.

Problem

It is 11:40pm and the driver thinks they paid. The old answers were log in, with no account, or call, with the lines closed.

Design

The check returns crossing status only, nothing personal, so it needs no login. Paid, due or not found, each with an honest next step.

Answers point 04

Check a crossing, paid

04 · A dashboard that speaks up

An account that cannot fail silently.

Problem

Accounts closed quietly and cards expired without warning, so people were fined with money still in the account.

Design

One card carries the account’s health: active, action needed, or suspended. Card expiry is warned at 30, 7 and 1 days, and auto top up is shown as a state.

Answers points 06, 07, 08, 11

Dashboard

05 · Pay or challenge, equally

Challenging a fine costs nothing, and returns something to track.

Problem

Appeals went into a paper form and a phone queue, with no reference, while the fine grew during the wait.

Design

The notice shows both amounts with their dates, and neither route is buried. A challenge returns a case reference and a 28 day reply window, and holds the reduced amount.

Answers points 13, 14, 15

Penalty notice

06 · No dead ends

Two dead ends, given a way out.

Problem

Cameras can take an hour to record a crossing, so “not found” read as “nothing owed”. An unmatched plate ended at an error.

Design

Pay in advance and match it later, a self declaration for plates the DVLA cannot match, a language switch, and help that leads with webchat.

Trade offs

What I turned down, each one cheaper to build.

One unified wizard for everyone
→
Split entry. Quick pay is the main action and accounts are a separate path. The wizard repeated the exact step where people gave up.
A live countdown timer
→
An exact date and time. A countdown is meaningless in a screenshot and unusable to a screen reader. “Due by 23:59, Sun 26 Jul” survives both.
Full account lookup by plate
→
Crossing status only. Showing nothing personal is what lets the check work without a login.
Fleet tools in the app
→
Left to the web. Nobody reconciles forty vehicles on a phone. Half serving fleets would have made the app worse for everyone.

Looking back

Two weeks went into the payment form. The real problem was that nobody knew a payment was due.

Do differently

The gap analysis should have come before any interface work, not after it.

Removing the hire car route was right. One line of copy should have replaced it.

Hardest call

Still right. Their work is desktop work, and the web service already serves it.

Heuristic and competitive review

Seven reds against none
+

Dart Charge scored red on seven of ten heuristics. The best comparator, The Toll Roads in the USA, scored red on none, which made the case that the failures were solvable rather than built into tolling.

  • Showstoppers: no way to know the email logs you in, no validation or verification, and no contact number anywhere on the site.
  • Major problems: errors that never say what went wrong, step counts never shown, and two names for the same task.
  • Irritants: buttons that move between screens, and data that is cluttered rather than grouped.

What people said when nobody was asking

Social media research
+

Interviews get considered answers. Unprompted complaints get the real ones. The five most common themes: not knowing the charge existed, missing the deadline, not being able to confirm a payment, being fined despite having an account, and paying a third party site by mistake.

Nobody wrote “the information architecture is unclear”. They wrote “where was I supposed to find this out”. That phrasing went straight into the landing screen.

Personas

Eight, built on behaviour. Two shown in full
+

Nobody wanted an account. Every practical need was informational, and three of the four touchpoints only appeared after something had gone wrong.

Journey and service maps

Where the severity peaks
+

The tallest pain point sits at the one step where the service has no way to reach the driver. Five days pass from compliant to enforcement, and the service speaks to the driver on none of them except through a letter at the end.

The seven gaps

What people needed, against what the service did
+
  1. Know a charge exists, and by when. The rule appeared in guidance, after the fact.
  2. Pay without opening an account. Account type came before a £2.50 payment.
  3. Confirm a payment landed. Impossible without logging in.
  4. Be told when the account cannot pay. Accounts closed quietly, people were fined in credit.
  5. Challenge a fine without it growing. A paper form, a phone queue, no reference.
  6. Pay when the plate is not recognised. A validation error and nothing after it.
  7. Get help without a queue. A phone number, opening hours, no idea of the wait.

Package A, the client recommendations

Five enhancements, all built in the concept
+
  1. Guidance on auto payments. Built as payment settings and the account health card.
  2. Well timed low balance alerts. Built as a banner the account state raises itself.
  3. Nominate a crossing. Built as step two of payment, tick the crossing.
  4. Pay for crossings ahead. Built as the crossing list and pay in advance.
  5. A dashboard by vehicle and period. Built as the dashboard and balance activity.

Five things review changed

Three rounds of critique against the HIG and GOV.UK standards
+
  1. Tab bar. A green pill behind the selected tab became tint and a filled icon, as iOS expects.
  2. Plate field. Yellow rear plates became the white front plate, the one people read standing in front of the car.
  3. Account type step. Deleted. A question with one answer is not a question.
  4. Low balance banner. Now raised by the account state itself, not switched on by hand.
  5. Hire car task. Removed, it duplicated ordinary payment.

What this research does not cover

Limits, stated plainly
+
  • The social media reading was pattern finding, not a survey, and carries no sample size.
  • The service map follows one journey type, a driver who crosses first and pays afterwards.
  • The research is from 2020. The penalty figures are 2024 reporting and show direction, not the conditions of the research.
