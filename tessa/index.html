Context

Operators weren't short of data. They were drowning in it.

Teams running integrations on TIBCO get far more alerts than anyone can read. The useful details sit in different tools, and nothing explains why a given alert matters. When everything is marked urgent, the alert that really matters gets missed.

Most

operators we spoke to muted or ignored alerts, because everything looked urgent.

Several

tools to piece together what happened in a single incident.

"Why?"

was the first question people asked of any AI suggestion. Without proof, they would not act on it.

Reframe

The brief said AI assistant. The real job was attention.

A chatbot dropped onto a noisy platform would be one more voice asking to be heard. So I gave every screen the same test: does this earn the operator's attention, or spend it?

She should notice the little things before they become big problems.

The line from the brief I kept coming back to.

Helpful, without becoming one more thing to read.

I led with action cards and suggested prompts instead of a blank chat box. Under pressure, people get a next step, not a blinking cursor.

Smart, but checkable.

Every answer links back to the data behind it. Agent Thoughts and a flow diagram are there for anyone who wants to look, and out of the way for anyone who does not.

How it changed

Week by week, with the people building it.

TESSA was not designed in isolation. I had regular calls with the product manager and the development team to pin down requirements and constraints: what users needed, what the agent could actually do, and what the platform allowed. Alongside those calls, we tested the designs with users, collected their feedback and redesigned every week, so each stakeholder review saw a finished, working version rather than a draft.

One decision worth explaining

Showing the working, without the noise.

The same answer has to serve two kinds of operator. Some want to check every step before they act on anything an AI tells them. Others, mid-incident, just want the answer. Showing the full reasoning under every reply would help the first group and bury the second.

So the reasoning sits behind a collapsed Agent Thoughts link on each answer, with a flow diagram for anyone who wants the full path. On top of that, an owner setting, Show TESSA Thoughts, turns it on or off for the whole team, and secrets are always redacted either way.

Trust for the people who need proof, and a clean answer for everyone else.

Two changes along the way, shown with real screens, not reconstructions.

From numbered sessions to conversations you can find again.

"Session 2" tells you nothing a week later. Investigations get picked up again, so in the later version every chat has a title, sits in a history panel and can be searched. The assistant also gets a name and its own place in the main navigation instead of a generic label.

The guided start shipped, regrouped by question.

The guided start made it into the product unchanged in shape: a greeting, one question box, topic chips and example prompts. What changed is how the chips are grouped. In the shipped version they follow the kind of question, like inventory, health check or anomaly detection, rather than product areas, and that is how the launch demo uses them.

Looking back

The hardest part wasn't making TESSA smart. It was making her feel quiet.

What I learned

Holding back was the real design work. Every time we wanted to add another number, alert, or animation, we asked if it was worth the user's attention. Usually it was not, and leaving it out made the important moments stand out more.

What I would change

I would focus on the "why" sooner. Showing how TESSA reaches an answer built the most trust, and it should have shaped the design from day one.
