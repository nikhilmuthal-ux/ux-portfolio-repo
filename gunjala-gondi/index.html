Prologue

A script without a font cannot be typed, searched or shared. It stays on paper, and slowly, on paper only, it fades. Gunjala Gondi was one of those scripts: written by hand in a small village in Telangana, and missing from every phone and computer.

In 2019 I joined EkType, one of India's leading type foundries, as a type design intern. I was part of the EkType team that designed what would become Noto Sans Gunjala Gondi, a Google font. My part was to learn the script, study how it had been written, and draw early drafts of letters for the regular weight, under the guidance of the team's type designers.

I could not read a single letter of it when I began.

II
𑶢

The place

The script is named after the village of Gunjala in the Adilabad district of Telangana, where manuscripts written in it were recently found.

Google's Noto project aims to give every writing system on earth a font, so that no language appears as empty boxes on a screen. Gunjala Gondi was one of the scripts still waiting. There was no earlier typeface to learn from, only the hands of the people who wrote it.

III
𑶣

Reading the hand

I learned the script the way you might learn someone's handwriting from their old letters: by copying it, comparing it, and noticing what stays the same from one page to the next.

i.

Where it beganCalligraphy practice

It started with calligraphy. Practising Devanagari with pen and ink taught me the gap between handwriting and type. A hand changes a little every time it writes a letter; a font has to give that letter one steady form that works in every word. Learning to see that difference is what I carried into Gunjala Gondi.

ii.

Primary researchLearning the letters by hand

I began with handwritten pages of the vowels, consonants and vowel signs, and wrote them out again and again until the shapes felt natural in my own hand.

iii.

First comparisonMapping every letter

These pages were written in Gunjala Gondi by a member of the community and carefully preserved by his family. I set them side by side and compared every vowel, consonant and number, to see where different writers agreed and where they drifted apart.

iv.

Secondary researchBack to the manuscripts

Everyone writes a little differently, so no single hand could simply be copied into a typeface. I studied the manuscripts from the village and took numbers, letters and writing style directly from them.

v.

Second comparisonEvery variant, one row per letter

With more references, I rebuilt the chart. Each row holds one letter and every version of it I found. The aim was not to pick one writer's style, but to find the clearest and most widely recognised form of each letter across all of them.

IV
𑶤

Drawing

Only after the research did I start to draw: by hand first, with pencil on gridded paper, then as clean outlines in FontLab, every letter sitting on the same set of guide lines.

A decision

Loop, or knot?

My two rounds of research gave me two honest versions of the same letter. The handwriting I learned first closes the letter KA with an open loop. The village manuscripts show a tighter knot.

This was not a choice to make on taste. A font is used by everyone who reads the script, so the right form is the one readers recognise most easily, not the one that looks best to the designer. It became one of the questions we carried to the people who read the script every day.

V
𑶥

The readers

A typeface only works if the people who read the script recognise every letter in it. I tested the drafts at several sizes for readability, and then we sat with fluent readers, sheets of options in hand, and asked them which forms looked right.

The people who use a design are the real experts on it.

The typeface

Together, the drafts grew into a complete regular weight: every vowel and consonant, and each consonant joined with every vowel sign.

𑶧

Afterword

I came to this project unable to read a single letter of Gunjala Gondi. The only way forward was the slow one: writing it again and again, and trusting the manuscripts over my own sense of what looked good.

The loop and the knot changed how I work. Every hand I studied wrote the script a little differently, and a typeface cannot copy any one of them. It has to hold the best of all of them. Sitting with fluent readers and letting them choose was the moment I understood who a design really belongs to.

If I did it again, I would bring readers in earlier, right after the first comparison chart, instead of waiting until the glyph set was drawn.

What I took away

Study how people actually write, compare every variation, and let the people who read it every day decide what is right.
